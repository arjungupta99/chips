Distribution

How Chips reaches people

Five pieces, four front doors, and one small server in the middle. Everything is free and every line is readable; the only thing that costs anything to run is the relay, and it costs almost nothing.

The pieces

Everything hangs off one server that runs on the user's own machine. The rest are windows onto it.

chipsd holds everything: messages, history, approvals, the event log. It runs on the machine the agents run on, so nothing leaves the house by default.

The browser page, the desktop app and the phone are three frames around one screen. The desktop app is a Tauri shell wrapping the same web build; the phone is Expo sharing the client core, not the components.

Where each piece comes from

Two you publish yourself. One goes through a store you do not control. One is not installed at all.

Ours

The server and CLI

A Homebrew formula and a curl installer, both already written in packaging/, plus docker compose up for teams in Phase 6. They need a tagged release to point at.

Ours

The desktop app

Tauri produces a .dmg and an .msi. Serve them from the site with auto-update. The Mac App Store is not worth the sandbox fight for a program that talks to local processes.

Theirs

The phone app

$99 a year to Apple, $25 once to Google, and review on every version. A free app with no in-app purchase pays no commission. The store is delivery, not discovery.

Neither

The relay

An address compiled into the software and printed into the pairing QR. Anyone who prefers their own runs it with one command.

A stranger's first install

Four steps from hearing about Chips to watching an agent work. No account, no password, no phone.

Nothing in that path involves a phone, a relay or a payment. She gets the full product from a browser on her own machine on day one; the desktop app and the phone are additions for people who want them.

The relay

The one piece we run. It exists because a phone on a train cannot dial a laptop behind a home router.

It is a postman who cannot read the letters. The encryption runs between the phone and the server; the relay forwards sealed bytes. That is not a promise but an acceptance test — a packet capture at the relay must show no plaintext.

It knows which server spoke to which phone and when, because a switchboard must. It should keep as little of that as it can, for as short a time as it can.

Many people, one relay

The relay does not join people together. It is a telephone exchange, not a shared building.

Two jobs that must stay apart. The relay decides who can be reached. Sarah's server decides what Tom may do — that is the authorization matrix, running on her machine, checking every request against his role. A relay that enforced a permission would have to read the traffic, and the privacy story would go with it.

Teammates are nearly free to carry: Tom adds a phone that connects only while his app is open, and no always-on server connection. The number that drives the bill is servers running Chips, not people using it.

  • Under a cent per user per month, because the relay stores nothing. No database, no files, no processing — each user's own machine does the work. A conventional app at this size runs $1,000 to $5,000 a month.
  • Where you host matters more than how many users you have. The same traffic costs twenty times more on AWS than on Hetzner. Do not put a bandwidth-shaped service on a bandwidth-priced host.

  • The real cost is not money. Two boxes, monitoring and someone to notice at 3am. Fifty dollars is nothing; being on the hook for uptime is the commitment.

Free, and open

For Chips the two are not a close call. Readable code is not a nicer kind of free — it is the distribution.

It holds their secrets

chipsd sits between people and their agents: tokens, code, approvals for money and deletions. The first thousand users are the most source-suspicious population alive. Free does not fix that; readable does.

Strangers must implement it

Agent Connect only wins if other people's harnesses speak it. Nobody implements a closed protocol from an unknown vendor, and no vendor's lawyer signs off without a patent grant. So the protocol, the SDK and the conformance kit are Apache-2.0.

It is how devtools travel

Homebrew, Docker Hub, npm, Hacker News, awesome-lists. Closed-but-free gets none of that engine, and you would be buying users instead of earning them.

What actually protects the business

Not the code alone. Neither licence grants rights to the name or the mark, so a fork must rename (TRADEMARKS.md). That is how Firefox, Grafana and Signal all do it, though their names are registered and ours is not, for now: see the names.

The server and apps are FSL-1.1-ALv2 (ADR 0128). Anyone can read, run and change them, a company can run Chips for its own people, and each version becomes Apache-2.0 two years after its release. What the licence refuses, until then, is someone else selling Chips as a product or a service — a company that would otherwise have to buy it included.

The line is drawn at the network boundary, not at features. Everything that runs on the user's machine is free and readable, and Apache-2.0 two years on. What we run for them — the relay and push fan-out, and no hosted workspaces for now — is the service. It never requires crippling the open build, which is what makes open-core feel grubby and stalls adoption.

The phone app is public too

Decided again on 16 October 2026. Free obliges nothing to be published, so the phone app could have stayed closed. It stays public under the FSL with the rest. Closing it would hide little: it shares packages/core with the web app, which ships readable in every install. It would also cost a repository split, and it would take away the one place people can read what happens to the approvals they give.

So we say “free and source available.” Only the protocol, the SDK and the conformance kit are open source until each version of the rest turns Apache-2.0. Calling the whole of Chips open source would be called out, and rightly.

Neither licence closes iOS to us. The GPL family conflicts with App Store terms, and going copyleft to protect the server would have got us blanket-banned in enterprise procurement later; the FSL does neither, and we publish the app ourselves.

npm @chipsA stranger'sAn npm user has owned it for about ten years, so @chips/… could never be ours. Hence @chipschat (ADR 0128, addendum).
Chips on the App StoreNot held yetNo exact match in nine storefronts on 6 October. The App Store Connect record reserves it, and comes with the first TestFlight upload.

Why not register now. A registration would not stop a renamed copy; that is the licence's job. Nor would it beat anyone who used the name first. What it buys is a filing date, and quick takedowns of copies that keep the name. The open field cuts both ways: anyone could still file first.

If it is ever wanted: CHIPS, on an intent-to-use basis, in classes 9 and 42, with narrow wording for the goods, about $700 at the USPTO. Not CHIPS CHAT, the form closest to chips.chat.

Nothing is public yet, by choice. Every piece works for one household. What stands between that and strangers is a list of his calls, not a rewrite:

  • The repository. A cleaned copy of the history, the contributor-agreement bot and a real security@chipschat.ai, then public.
  • The release. Published on the public repository's GitHub releases, which install.sh downloads from, with the Homebrew formula pointed at it.
  • The phone app. The TestFlight upload, which also reserves the App Store name.
  • The Mac app. Developer ID signing and notarization.
  • The relay. Seven-day log rotation, then other servers' keys on its allowlist.
  • npm. @chipschat/harness, /protocol, /conformance and the connectors published, each then added to the gallery's published list.
  • The name. The USPTO search run again.
